I reviewed the uploaded PDF . My overall view is that this is **not** referee-ready. The finite-level combinatorics and computations may contain some real arithmetic observations, but the paper’s central infinite-dimensional operator theory on (C(\mathbf Z_2^{\mathrm{odd}})) has foundational errors. The biggest one is that it repeatedly treats reduction mod (3) as a meaningful continuous notion on (\mathbf Z_2); it is not. That single mistake infects the claimed preimage structure, the norm computation, the Lasota–Yorke obstruction, and much of the spectral discussion.

### Abstract

The abstract claims a lot: (|L|=2/3), (\rho(L)\le 1/2), two operator-theoretic obstructions, persistence of ghost cycles, positive density of exceptional levels, a census of materializing ghost types, and Theorem 6 on negative rationality for concentrated patterns. As a summary of what the paper *intends* to do, it is accurate. As a summary of what is actually established, it overstates matters badly, because several of the “proved” spectral/operator claims rely on a broken model of the transfer operator on (\mathbf Z_2^{\mathrm{odd}}). The computational claims are more believable than the operator-theoretic ones, and Theorem 6 may well be correct, but the abstract does not distinguish clearly between rigorous finite-level results, plausible but underproved statements, and large conjectural extrapolations. A referee would almost certainly ask for the abstract to be rewritten in a much more cautious way. 

### Introduction

The introduction frames the paper around a “central discovery”: ghost cycles are said to be genuine 2-adic periodic orbits, with case-(a) ghosts recurring in arithmetic progressions of levels, falsifying an earlier density-zero conjecture. It also lists a long menu of contributions, ranging from the finite transfer matrices (P_k) to operator norms, spectral theorems, density bounds, and replacement conjectures. That is a coherent research program, but the introduction presents it with much more certainty than the paper has earned. In particular, the phrases “closing the Mahler/Amice program entirely” and “central discovery” are too strong when the operator (L) itself is not soundly set up on the stated space. A referee would want this section toned down and split more sharply between finite-level computations, proved arithmetic statements, and speculative infinite-level operator theory.

### Transfer Matrix (Section 2 / finite-level setup)

This is one of the stronger parts. The finite-level objects are clear: the modular Syracuse map (S_k), the weighted transfer matrix (P_k), the exceptional set (E), and the formula for the spectral radius of (P_k) in terms of weighted cycles. The valuation-distribution proposition is standard and essentially correct. At the level of finite weighted functional graphs, the paper is on firmer ground.

The main referee concern here is not the finite definitions themselves, but what comes next. Section 2 sets up a clean finite combinatorial problem, but the later paper implicitly treats those matrices as though they came from a well-defined transfer operator on continuous 2-adic functions with the same arithmetic preimage structure. That passage is not justified here. I would urge a referee to insist that the paper separate “finite-level weighted graph facts” from “infinite-level operator facts,” because the former are substantially more credible than the latter. 

### Spectral Theory (Sections 3–5)

This is the weakest part of the paper. The fatal issue appears immediately in Lemma 1 and Proposition 2: the paper classifies odd 2-adic integers by their residue mod (3), and from that derives the preimage structure and the weight function (W=L1). But “(n \bmod 3)” is not a well-defined continuous invariant of (n\in \mathbf Z_2). A concrete counterexample is (n=1): the paper says only even (v) should occur in that case, yet (m=(2\cdot 1-1)/3=1/3) is an odd 2-adic preimage with (v=1). So Lemma 1 is false on its face. Once that fails, the claimed formula (W\in{0,1/3,2/3}), the norm (|L|=2/3), and the entire mod-3 oscillation mechanism of Theorem 2 collapse. The paper even states that residue classes mod (3) are clopen in (\mathbf Z_2); that is simply wrong.

A second major error is Theorem 1(c): the paper uses (\delta_1=1_{{1}}) as a continuous eigenfunction on (\mathbf Z_2^{\mathrm{odd}}). But ({1}) is not open in (\mathbf Z_2), so its indicator is not continuous. Theorem 1(e), asserting (\sigma(L)=\bigcup_k \sigma(P_k)), is also not proved: density of locally constant functions does not identify the full spectrum of an infinite-dimensional operator with the spectra of finite truncations. Theorem 3 on 2-adic unboundedness may be pointing at a real phenomenon, but as written it is built on an operator that has not been correctly formulated. A referee would likely require Sections 3–5 to be completely rewritten, and I suspect most of the infinite-dimensional spectral claims would have to be withdrawn unless the author can rebuild the theory on a genuinely correct space/operator.

### Ghost Cycles (Sections 6 and 7.1–7.2)

The finite-level ghost-cycle material is more promising. The exhaustive search through (k=36), summarized on page 9, is concrete; the chord diagrams are a useful visualization of when extra modular cycles appear; and the cycle equation in Theorem 4 is standard Collatz-periodic-orbit algebra. The case-(a)/case-(b) distinction is also a sensible organizing device: it cleanly separates valuation patterns that exactly match the rational orbit from those with extra 2-adic cancellation. If the paper had confined itself to this finite-level and rational-orbit arithmetic, it would have been much stronger.

The problem is that the paper repeatedly slides from “we found modular cycles and associated rational 2-adic periodic points” to “ghost cycles are not transient artifacts; they are true 2-adic periodic orbits” as though this were a global theorem. What is actually shown is narrower: some explicitly checked patterns are case-(a), and many more are sampled computationally. The “88+ materializing ghost types” claim is explicitly only a lower bound because many non-concentrated patterns were sampled rather than exhausted. Also, the Baker–Wüstholz subsection is sloppy: the claim that the lower bound on (|2^V-3^L|) implies (|D|) grows “superexponentially in (V)” is false as stated. A referee would likely ask the author to recast this entire part as a mixture of exact finite enumeration, exact algebraic identities, and computational evidence, rather than as a finished structural classification.

### Persistence (Theorem 5)

The persistence theorem is plausible and important: it says a case-(a) ghost, once it appears, reappears on an arithmetic progression of levels with period (\operatorname{ord}_2(|D|)). The figure on page 16 is visually consistent with that claim. If correct, it is exactly the sort of theorem that can turn a sporadic computational observation into a genuine infinite family. 

But the proof is not good enough. The crucial sentence is that (D^{-1}) has a (p)-periodic 2-adic expansion, so the reduction (\tilde n_i \bmod 2^k) depends only on (k \bmod p). That is not stated precisely enough to be accepted; truncations modulo (2^k) do not literally depend only on (k \bmod p), and one has to prove that appending one full period of binary digits preserves all required valuation conditions. As written, this reads like a proof sketch, not a theorem. Because the density lower bound depends on it, a referee would insist on a careful standalone lemma about periodic 2-adic expansions of rationals with odd denominator and a rigorous deduction of the recurrence-in-(k) statement.

### Density (Sections 9.1–9.2)

The one solid-looking density claim is the lower bound (\delta(E)\ge 1/25=4%), coming from the explicitly identified (D=-601) ghost if Theorem 5 is valid. That is the right kind of statement: concrete, arithmetically explicit, and modest. Everything beyond that is much less secure. The product formula for (\delta(E)) is explicitly acknowledged to need inclusion-exclusion corrections; the census of ghost types is incomplete; and the empirical densities up to (k=1000) are nowhere near enough to support a serious claim about an exact natural density, especially when the relevant periods have enormous least common multiples.

So a referee concern here is not that the conjectural discussion is illegitimate, but that the paper does not control its rhetoric. The difference between “here is a rigorous 4% lower bound from one family” and “the density seems to be around 10%” is enormous, and the latter remains heuristic. I would ask the author to present only the 4% bound as a theorem and move the rest into a clearly marked heuristic subsection.

### Spectral Radius (Section 9.3)

As a statement about the finite matrices (P_k), this section has a reasonable core: explicit materializing ghost families give explicit lower bounds on (\limsup_k \rho_k), and the universal upper bound (\rho_k\le 1/2) is immediate from (V\ge L). The observed family with (L=15,V=16) does give the lower bound (2^{-16/15}\approx 0.4774), and the heuristic that fixed-excess families push toward (1/2) is natural.

The trouble is that the section blurs three different objects: (\rho_k), (\limsup_k \rho_k), and the spectral radius of the infinite operator (L). Because the operator theory in Sections 3–5 is not secure, statements about (\rho(L)) are not presently justified. A referee should demand that this section be rewritten purely in finite-level terms unless and until the transfer-operator framework is repaired.

### Negative Rationality (Conjecture 4 and Theorem 6)

This is, in my view, one of the paper’s better sections. Conjecture 4 is clear, and Theorem 6 gives an explicit closed form for the orbit numerators in the concentrated family ((1,\dots,1,e+1)). The recurrence-solving argument is straightforward, and the positivity/oddness checks look plausible. Unlike the earlier operator-theoretic sections, this part does not seem to rely on the broken “mod 3 on (\mathbf Z_2)” framework. So if I were refereeing, I would treat Theorem 6 as potentially salvageable mathematics that could survive major revisions elsewhere.

The objection is one of scope and interpretation. The paper leans very heavily on this theorem as “strong evidence” about the whole high-spectral-radius regime, but the theorem handles only concentrated patterns, and the computational verification through (L=12) is still just computation. It is interesting evidence, not a structural classification. A referee would likely ask the author to keep the theorem, but drastically reduce the rhetoric around what it means for the Collatz problem or for the full ghost-cycle landscape.

### Fredholm Determinant

This section is mathematically harmless but overstated. For non-exceptional finite matrices (P_k), the statement (\det(I-zP_k)=1-z/4) is just the characteristic-polynomial computation for a matrix whose only nonzero eigenvalue is (1/4). That is fine. What it is *not* is a serious Fredholm-determinant theory for the infinite operator (L). No infinite-dimensional determinant is defined, no analytic properties are studied, and the preceding operator theory is too shaky to support one anyway.

A referee would probably ask either that this be folded into the finite spectral-computation section or that the title be changed. Also, because (P_k) comes from an explicit weighted functional graph, the nonzero eigenvalues can be read off exactly from the cycle decomposition; floating-point dense eigensolvers are not really the right tool for the core finite-level statements.

### Related Work

The related-work paragraph cites the expected classical sources and several recent operator-theoretic or p-adic papers. That is enough to show basic awareness, and the comparisons to Steiner, Wirsching, Lagarias, Tao, and Simons–de Weger are broadly sensible. But the engagement is shallow. The paper does not really situate itself within the broader literature on dynamics or transfer operators on totally disconnected spaces, which is exactly where its topological mistakes would have become obvious.

The more serious referee concern is tone. Claims like “our analysis identifies two independent obstructions that apply to all such approaches” are too sweeping given the paper’s own unresolved foundational problems. I would want this section rewritten in a more modest register, with a clearer distinction between what is genuinely new and what is only a computational rephrasing of older Collatz-periodic-orbit algebra.

### Discussion / Conclusion

The discussion is the most speculative part of the paper. It talks about archimedean compactness, essential spectrum, (\sigma(L)\supset [1/4,1/2]), inducing schemes, an Iwasawa-theoretic analogy, and connections to recent work. As a research notebook or agenda, that is fine. As the conclusion of a paper whose central operator theory is not yet correct, it is too ambitious. Many of the discussion points lean directly on Theorem 1 and the mod-3 obstruction, both of which are not presently reliable.

If I were the referee, I would recommend a major reframing: strip the conclusion down to the robust finite-level facts, the exact cycle equation, the arithmetic evidence for persistence, and Theorem 6 if it checks out. The current conclusion reads as though a coherent transfer-operator theory has been established and then obstructed; I do not think the paper has reached that point.

My bottom-line referee judgment would be: **interesting computational and arithmetic observations, but not publishable in current form because the central infinite-dimensional 2-adic operator framework is mathematically unsound as written.**
