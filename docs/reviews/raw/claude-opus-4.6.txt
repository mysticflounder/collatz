## Abstract

**What it claims:** The paper studies the Syracuse map via its transfer operator $L$ on $C(\mathbb{Z}_2^{\text{odd}})$ and finite approximations $P_k$. It claims $\|L\| = 2/3$, $\rho(L) \leq 1/2$, a Lasota–Yorke obstruction, exhaustive ghost cycle enumeration through $k=36$, identification of ghosts as 2-adic periodic orbits, density $\geq 4\%$ for the exceptional set, 88+ materializing ghost types, and four replacement conjectures.

**Assessment:** The abstract is dense but accurately reflects the paper's contents. The scope is well-calibrated — it explicitly does not claim to prove the Collatz conjecture. However, a referee would note that the abstract buries the lede: the most striking claim (that the exceptional set $E$ is infinite with positive density, falsifying a prior conjecture) should be foregrounded. The phrase "replacement conjectures" is also unclear without context — replacement for what? The abstract assumes the reader knows the author's prior work. The claim of "88+ materializing ghost types" is a lower bound from sampling, not an exact count, and this deserves more careful phrasing even in the abstract.

---

## Introduction

**What it claims:** Frames the paper as studying the Collatz problem through transfer matrices/operators. Lists seven contributions, cites related work, and provides an outline.

**Assessment:** The introduction is competently structured. The related work paragraph is a strength — it places the paper relative to Tao, Lagarias–Weiss, Steiner, Siegel, etc. A referee concern: the paper claims to "falsify" the conjecture that $E$ has density zero, but that conjecture appears to be the author's own from a prior version of this work, not a published conjecture by an independent researcher. "Falsifying our own earlier conjecture" is honest but has less impact than the framing suggests. The claim that this work "extends prior searches" in cycle enumeration needs a concrete comparison — what was the previous state of the art for exhaustive $k$? The mention of a "companion paper" on the parametric family is a loose thread; if unpublished, it weakens claims about the framework's generality. The URL to the code repository is good practice.

## Transfer Matrix (Section 2: Definitions and Setup)

**What it claims:** Defines the Syracuse map, modular Syracuse map, transfer matrix $P_k$, spectral radius, exceptional set, and proves the valuation distribution (Proposition 1: $P(v_2(3n+1)=j) = 2^{-j}$, $\mathbb{E}[v]=2$).

**Assessment:** The definitions are standard and correctly stated. The transfer matrix definition (Definition 3) is clear — each column has one nonzero entry, making $P_k$ a weighted permutation-like matrix. The claim that eigenvalues are $L$th roots of $2^{-V}$ follows from the block structure of weighted permutation matrices restricted to cyclic components — this is correct. Proposition 1 is folklore, as acknowledged, and the proof sketch is adequate. One concern: the proof of the valuation distribution has a minor expository hiccup — it first states $P(v=j) = 1/2^{j+1}$ then corrects to $1/2^j$, which is confusing. The correct answer is $P(v=j) = 2^{-j}$ for $j \geq 1$, and the proof should state this cleanly. Also, the claim that $\rho_k = 1/4$ at non-exceptional levels assumes the fixed point $\{1\}$ always appears, which is true (since $S(1)=1$ in all residue systems) but should be stated explicitly.

## Spectral Theory (Section 3: Transfer Operator on $\mathbb{Z}_2^{\text{odd}}$)

**What it claims:** Develops $L$ on $C(\mathbb{Z}_2^{\text{odd}})$: Lemma 1 (preimage structure), Proposition 2 ($\|L\| = 2/3$), Proposition 3 ($\rho(L) \leq 1/2$), and Theorem 1 (six-part spectral theorem).

**Assessment:** Lemma 1 is correct — the preimage enumeration by residue class mod 3 is standard. The operator norm computation (Proposition 2) is clean and correct: the geometric series for even/odd $v$ give $1/3$ and $2/3$ respectively. The remark about correcting an earlier error ($1/3$ vs $2/3$) is commendable transparency.

The spectral radius bound (Proposition 3) has a significant logical gap. The proof asserts "every eigenvalue of $L$ corresponds to a periodic orbit of $S$" — but this needs justification. For a general bounded operator on $C(X)$, the spectrum need not consist only of eigenvalues. The paper then invokes Theorem 1(e), which states $\sigma(L) = \overline{\bigcup \sigma(P_k)}$, but the proof of 1(e) itself is the weakest part of the theorem. The argument that eigenvalues of $P_k$ are approximate eigenvalues of $L$ is plausible but hand-wavy: it claims the coarsening error "vanishes in the projective limit" without quantifying this. The converse direction uses Stone–Weierstrass density, which gives approximate eigenvectors but doesn't rigorously establish the spectral identity without more careful functional analysis (e.g., strong convergence of resolvents). A referee would likely ask for either a more careful proof or a citation to a general result on projective limits of operators.

Part (c) claiming $\lambda = 1/4$ is simple is justified only computationally ("no other cycle with $\prod 2^{-v_i} = 1/4$ materializes at any level $k \leq 36$"). This is evidence, not proof. A cycle with $V = 2L$ and any $L$ would produce eigenvalue $1/4$, and the paper has no proof that such cycles can't materialize at large $k$.

## Lasota–Yorke Obstruction (Section 4: Theorem 2)

**What it claims:** $L$ does not preserve $\text{Lip}_1(\mathbb{Z}_2^{\text{odd}})$ or any Hölder/BV/Sobolev space defined via the 2-adic metric, because $W = L(\mathbf{1})$ has infinite Lipschitz seminorm.

**Assessment:** This is the strongest purely mathematical contribution. The proof is elegant and correct: taking $x_N = 1$, $y_N = 1 + 2^N$ with $N$ even gives $W(x_N) = 1/3$, $W(y_N) = 2/3$ while $|x_N - y_N|_2 = 2^{-N}$, so the Lipschitz ratio diverges. Corollary 1 extending this to all Hölder and modulus-of-continuity spaces is immediate and correct.

The root-cause remark (mod-3 oscillation) is insightful: since $\mathbb{Z}/3\mathbb{Z}$-class membership is continuous but not Lipschitz in the 2-adic metric, any weight function depending on mod-3 residues will fail. This is a genuine structural observation.

A referee concern: the paper frames this as blocking "the Lasota–Yorke approach," but the result is specifically about 2-adic regularity spaces. The paper acknowledges this ("obstructing the Lasota–Yorke approach... when using 2-adic regularity") but could be more explicit that the obstruction might not apply to other function spaces (e.g., Mahler basis expansions, as the Discussion hints). The result shows $L$ doesn't map Lip into Lip, but this doesn't preclude a Lasota–Yorke inequality on some other Banach space that embeds into $C(\mathbb{Z}_2^{\text{odd}})$ without being defined by a 2-adic modulus of continuity.

## Ghost Cycles (Section 6: Ghost Cycles as 2-Adic Periodic Orbits)

**What it claims:** Theorem 3 (cycle equation, attributed to Steiner/Wirsching). Definition 6 (case-(a) vs case-(b) classification). Conjecture 1 (universal case-(a)).

**Assessment:** The cycle equation is correctly stated and is indeed classical (Steiner 1977). The derivation is standard. The case-(a)/(b) classification is a useful organizational tool: case-(a) ghosts correspond to true 2-adic periodic orbits, case-(b) ghosts are artifacts of insufficient resolution.

Conjecture 1 (universal case-(a)) is ambitious: it claims every composition of $V$ into $L$ parts with $V < 2L$ gives a case-(a) ghost. The evidence is computational — exhaustive through $L = 12$, sampled through $L = 20$. This is substantial evidence but a referee would note: (i) the conjecture covers infinitely many $(L,V)$ pairs and no theoretical argument is offered for why case-(b) should never occur in this range; (ii) the extension to $V \geq 2L$ is mentioned as also holding, which makes the restriction to $V < 2L$ in the conjecture statement seem arbitrary; (iii) the conjecture would benefit from at least a heuristic argument for *why* it should be true, beyond "we checked a lot of cases."

## Persistence (Theorem 4)

**What it claims:** Case-(a) ghosts reappear at arithmetic progressions of levels with period $p = \text{ord}_2(|D|)$.

**Assessment:** The proof is correct and is the paper's key structural result. The argument is clean: the 2-adic expansion of $R/D$ is periodic with period $p$, so the modular reduction $\tilde{n}_1 \bmod 2^k$ depends only on $k \bmod p$. Since valuation conditions are checked against finitely many bits, they're stable once $k$ is large enough.

A referee concern: the proof assumes $k > v_i$ for all $i$ (condition (ii)), which means the persistence only kicks in for $k \geq k_0$ where $k_0$ is large enough. The theorem states "reappears at all levels $k \equiv k_0 \pmod{p}$ with $k \geq k_0$" but the proof doesn't carefully establish that $k_0$ is the *first* appearance — it only shows periodicity for sufficiently large $k$. The gap between "first appearance" and "periodic from the first appearance" is small but should be addressed. Also, the claim that $D^{-1} \bmod 2^k$ is periodic in $k$ with period dividing $p = \text{ord}_2(|D|)$ conflates two things: the multiplicative order of 2 modulo $|D|$ governs the periodicity of $2^k \bmod |D|$, not directly $D^{-1} \bmod 2^k$. The conclusion is correct (the 2-adic expansion of $1/D$ is eventually periodic with period $p$) but the reasoning could be stated more precisely.

## Density (Section 8)

**What it claims:** The exceptional set has density $\geq 4\%$ unconditionally (from the $D = -601$ ghost alone, period 25). Conjecture 2 proposes an inclusion-exclusion formula. Empirical density is around 10%.

**Assessment:** The unconditional lower bound $\delta(E) \geq 1/25 = 4\%$ is correct, given Theorem 4 and the verified existence of the $D=-601$ ghost at $k=12$. This is a clean, meaningful result.

Conjecture 2's product formula treats different ghost types as independent events in a Chinese Remainder Theorem sense, which requires coprime periods. The paper acknowledges the periods are not coprime and calls the product a lower bound, which is correct. The empirical density scan through $k=1000$ shows reasonable agreement. However, a referee would observe: (i) the "conjecture" is really a formula with an explicit error term (non-coprime corrections), making it more of a heuristic estimate than a conjecture; (ii) the empirical check only goes to $k=1000$, while $\text{lcm}(\text{periods}) \sim 10^{10}$, so convergence is not demonstrated; (iii) the paper only accounts for ghost types with $L \leq 12$ — larger ghosts could change the density, and the conjecture implicitly assumes their cumulative contribution converges. No argument for convergence of the infinite product is given.

## Spectral Radius (Conjecture 3)

**What it claims:** $\limsup \rho_k = 1/2$, with proved lower bound $2^{-16/15} \approx 0.4774$ from the $L=15$, $V=16$ ghost.

**Assessment:** The proved lower bound is correct — the $L=15$ ghost is verified to materialize. Conjecture 3 is well-motivated: the $V = L+1$ family gives $\rho \to 1/2$ and materializing ghosts appear at most tested $L$ values. The gap at $L = 9, 11$ (no materialization despite being case-(a)) is honestly reported.

A referee concern: the conjecture $\limsup \rho_k = 1/2$ is really asking whether infinitely many ghost families materialize with $V/L \to 1$. The $V = L+1$ family alone suffices if it materializes for infinitely many $L$, but the paper cannot prove this. The equidistribution heuristic ($p/2^L$ predicts materialization count) is plausible but unproven. Also, if $\rho(L) = 1/2$, then the paper should discuss more carefully what this means: $1/2$ is still less than 1, so $L$ is still a contraction, just not as strong as hoped. The implications for the Collatz conjecture itself are unclear and could be discussed.

## Negative Rationality (Conjecture 4 + Theorem 6)

**What it claims:** All case-(a) ghost orbits with $D < 0$ have negative rational elements ($R_i > 0$ for all $i$, so $\tilde{n}_i = R_i/D < 0$). The remark notes this implies nonexistence of non-trivial positive-integer Collatz cycles.

**Assessment:** This is the most consequential conjecture — if proved, it would resolve the periodic orbit part of the Collatz conjecture. The computational evidence (all 5,996 canonical types through $L=12$) is solid.

A referee would raise serious concerns: (i) the implication for Collatz cycles is stated in a remark rather than a theorem, perhaps to avoid overclaiming, but it deserves a more careful treatment — specifically, the paper should verify that any positive-integer cycle would indeed have $D < 0$ (this requires $V < L \log_2 3 \approx 1.585L$, which is true for $V \leq 2L-1$ only when $L$ is small; for large $L$, cycles with $V/L$ near $\log_2 3$ would have $D$ near zero); (ii) the conjecture is purely empirical with no theoretical explanation for *why* all $R_i$ should be positive; (iii) existing results on the nonexistence of non-trivial Collatz cycles (e.g., Eliahou 1993 showing no cycle of length $< 17{,}087{,}915$) are much stronger than what Conjecture 4 provides computationally; (iv) the conjecture says nothing about divergent trajectories, which is the harder part of the Collatz conjecture.

## Fredholm Determinant (Section 9: Eigenvalue Spectra)

**What it claims:** Dense eigenvalue computation for $k = 3, \ldots, 15$ shows non-exceptional $P_k$ have spectrum exactly $\{0, 1/4\}$. The Fredholm determinant for non-exceptional $k$ is $\det(I - zP_k) = 1 - z/4$.

**Assessment:** The eigenvalue table is clean and the nonzero eigenvalue counts are correctly explained by cycle structure. The observation that non-exceptional $P_k$ are essentially nilpotent (rank 1) is interesting.

Referee concerns: (i) the section title in the outline says "Eigenvalue Spectra" but the Fredholm determinant claim $\det(I - zP_k) = 1 - z/4$ is stated without proof — for an $N \times N$ matrix with one nonzero eigenvalue $1/4$, the determinant should be $(1 - z/4) \cdot 1^{N-1} = 1 - z/4$, which is correct but only for the polynomial truncation; the full Fredholm determinant of the infinite-dimensional operator $L$ is not defined here; (ii) the warning about sparse eigensolver artifacts is useful methodologically but suggests the numerical results need care — the paper should confirm that the dense eigenvalues at exceptional $k$ are not also subject to numerical issues (e.g., by computing them in exact arithmetic or with interval arithmetic); (iii) this section feels underdeveloped — the Fredholm determinant is mentioned but never used for anything. If it's not driving any result, it could be cut.

## Related Work

**What it claims:** Embedded in the Introduction. Credits Matthews–Watts, Wirsching, Lagarias–Weiss, Tao, Steiner, Siegel, Baker–Wüstholz, Kontorovich–Lagarias, Assani, Mori, Neklyudov.

**Assessment:** The coverage is good for a preprint — major relevant works are cited. The paper distinguishes itself from Siegel (2025a) clearly. The positioning relative to Tao (2022) is appropriate (different approach, different results).

A referee would note: (i) the Steiner (1977) reference for Theorem 3 is hard to verify — the proceedings are obscure, and the result is sometimes attributed differently; (ii) the paper doesn't cite Eliahou (1993) or other cycle-exclusion results, which is a significant gap given Conjecture 4's implications; (iii) the Kontorovich–Lagarias citation is relevant but the paper doesn't explain how its density results compare to theirs; (iv) Bohm and Sontacchi (1978) on the cycle equation should be cited alongside Steiner.

## Discussion/Conclusion

**What it claims:** Discusses salvage approaches (mod-3 restriction, Mahler basis, Iwasawa algebra, thermodynamic formalism), the possibility that $\sigma(L) \supseteq [1/4, 1/2]$, and limitations of Baker–Wüstholz bounds.

**Assessment:** The discussion is intellectually honest — it clearly states the paper does not address the Collatz conjecture directly. The suggestion that $\sigma(L)$ might contain the full interval $[1/4, 1/2]$ (which would mean no spectral gap) is the paper's most provocative conclusion, properly hedged as conditional on conjectures.

Referee concerns: (i) the Iwasawa analogy ("infinitude of $E$ corresponds to $\mu \neq 0$") is stated in one sentence with no justification — this is either a deep observation or a superficial analogy, and the paper doesn't give the reader enough to judge; (ii) the Mahler basis suggestion is vague — has the author computed the action of $L$ on any Mahler basis elements?; (iii) the paper ends without a clear statement of what the most important open problem is from this work's perspective; (iv) the acknowledgment of Claude for "code development and computational exploration" is fine but a referee might ask about the reliability of AI-assisted mathematical reasoning in this context.

---

## Overall Assessment

The paper's strongest contributions are Theorem 2 (Lasota–Yorke obstruction), Theorem 4 (persistence of case-(a) ghosts), and the unconditional density lower bound. These are genuine mathematical results, correctly proved. The computational census is thorough and the falsification of the density-zero conjecture is convincing.

The weakest points are: (1) the proof of Theorem 1(e) (spectral identity via projective limit), which needs more rigor; (2) the four conjectures, which are empirically motivated but lack any theoretical supporting argument; (3) the simplicity claim for $\lambda = 1/4$ in Theorem 1(c), which is computational, not proved; and (4) the Fredholm determinant material, which is underdeveloped. The paper is honest about what is proved vs. conjectured, which is a significant virtue in Collatz-adjacent work. A referee would likely request tightening of the spectral theory proofs and better contextualization of how this work advances the state of the art beyond what was already known about 2-adic Collatz dynamics.
